\begin{abstract}
Self-training loops (generate $\rightarrow$ verify $\rightarrow$ select
$\rightarrow$ train, GVT) assume a reliable verifier. We tested whether a
rule-based verifier with a directional format bias --- \texttt{math-verify}
rejects \verb|\dfrac|/\verb|\tfrac| rewrites of correct answers $100\%$ of the
time --- causes capability collapse over rounds. A pooled GSM8K\,+\,MATH-500 run
appeared to confirm it (Qwen2.5-1.5B pass@8 $72.6\%\to48.6\%$), but it shared one
adapter across datasets and scored on the selection items. With isolated
adapters, a held-out exam split and a matched patched-verifier control, the
collapse disappears at our training rate ($10^{-5}$) and patching changes nothing
across two paired seeds: the biased format is only $\approx0.55\%$ of generations,
so impact is severity $\times$ prevalence. A late check qualifies this: $10^{-5}$
barely trains the adapter. At $10^{-4}$ (three seeds) accuracy falls by about two
points, within noise, but the loss grows with the learning rate and at
$5\times10^{-4}$ all four runs lose accuracy by round~2. The patch's effect flips
sign between seeds, and no-filter and train-on-gold controls (two seeds each) show the verifier is
not the driver. The decline has a different anatomy in each regime: under
self-training, generations that drift and never answer rise from about $10\%$ to
$40\%$ at $5\times10^{-4}$ while accuracy on finished solutions holds, and a larger
token cap does not remove it, since the untrained model gains as much from it;
under reference-solution training, responses halve in length and accuracy truly
falls.

\keywords{self-training \and verifier bias \and mathematical reasoning
\and small language models \and experimental confounds \and pass@k.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A common recipe for improving the mathematical reasoning of a small language
model (SLM) \emph{without human annotation} is to let the model generate its own
solutions, keep those that an automated verifier judges correct, and fine-tune
on the kept solutions, for several rounds. We call this the
\emph{Generate--Verify--Train} (GVT) loop; it underlies STaR~\cite{ref_star},
ReST~\cite{ref_rest}, ReST-EM~\cite{ref_restem} and rejection-sampling
fine-tuning~\cite{ref_rft}. Every such method assumes that the verifier separates
correct from incorrect solutions reliably, which a \emph{rule-based} verifier
such as \texttt{math-verify}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/huggingface/math-verify},
version 0.9.0 with antlr4 4.13.2 in this work.} violates in one direction: it
compares an extracted final answer with the gold answer after normalisation, so
it almost never accepts a wrong answer but rejects correct answers written in
forms it does not recognise. We measured this bias directly
(Section~\ref{sec:verifier}): on GSM8K the false-negative rate on correct
solutions is effectively zero, on MATH-500 it is about $7.8\%$, and some valid
rewrites, such as \verb|\dfrac| for \verb|\frac|, are rejected $100\%$ of the time.

This paper asks a single question: \emph{when a directionally biased verifier is
the filter in a multi-round GVT loop, does the model improve, merely learn the
verifier's preferred style, or lose capability?} Our first experiments seemed to
answer it decisively. Five rounds on a pooled GSM8K\,+\,MATH-500 set cut pass@1
and pass@8 sharply for two model sizes, and an earlier version of this work
reported this as evidence that format-biased verifiers cause collapse. It does
not survive a controlled re-examination. The pooled design trains \emph{one}
shared adapter on both datasets (GSM8K supplies about $69\%$ of the mixture) and
scores on the very items used for selection. Rebuilt with one adapter per
dataset, a held-out $30\%$ exam split and a matched patched-verifier control, the
collapse disappears and the control is null, at the learning rate we used
($10^{-5}$). A late diagnostic then showed that rate to be too low to train the
adapter at all, and the rest of the paper re-examines the result at rates where
the model does learn.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We characterise the directional bias of a real rule-based verifier by
format probe and manual false-negative audit, and show that its impact is
severity $\times$ prevalence: $100\%$ rejection of a format that occurs in
$\approx0.55\%$ of generations discards half a percent of the training pool.
\item We show that a pooled design with selection-set scoring manufactures a
collapse that isolation and held-out evaluation do not reproduce, and that the
matched patched-verifier control is null at two seeds.
\item We show that this flatness is conditional on the learning rate: $10^{-5}$
barely trains the adapter, accuracy at $10^{-4}$ falls by about two points
(within noise, three seeds), and the loss grows with the rate, reaching a
decline at $5\times10^{-4}$. The patch's effect has no consistent sign across
seeds, and no-filter and train-on-gold controls show the verifier is not the
driver.
\item We decompose the remaining decline and relate it to two recent studies
(Section~\ref{sec:related}): under self-training it is mostly generations that
drift and never answer, with accuracy on finished solutions unchanged; under
reference-solution training it is shortened responses and a real loss of accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Self-training and its limits.}
STaR~\cite{ref_star} introduced the generate--filter--retrain loop, and
ReST~\cite{ref_rest}, ReST-EM~\cite{ref_restem} and RFT~\cite{ref_rft} formalise
it; ReST-EM reports diminishing test gains after the first round and an
uninvestigated one-round regression on APPS. All treat the correctness signal as
trustworthy. Yue et al.~\cite{ref_rllimit} show that RL raises pass@1 while the
base model wins at large pass@$k$, and Teacher-Free Self-Training~\cite{ref_teacherfree}
observes a pass@8 versus pass@64 crossover on a free-verifier domain, concluding
that self-training ``amplifies but does not compound''. Training repeatedly on
model-generated data erodes distribution tails~\cite{ref_collapse}, and Qiao et
al.~\cite{ref_selectionbias} argue theoretically that a selector seeing only a
narrow slice of the target distribution accelerates collapse.

\paragraph{Verifier error.}
TinyV~\cite{ref_tinyv} measures that rule-based verifiers reject many correct
solutions and repairs them with an LLM check; From Accuracy to
Robustness~\cite{ref_robustness} reports about $14\%$ false negatives for
rule-based verifiers in mathematical RL, consistent with ours. Imperfect
Verifiers~\cite{ref_imperfect} gives the most formal noise model, with fixed
false-accept and false-reject probabilities, and notes that real noise can depend
on formatting but then assumes content-independent rates in a single RL round.
Egashira et al.~\cite{ref_delayplateau} classify verifier errors in RLVR on a
synthetic arithmetic task (Qwen3-1.7B-Base, OLMo3-7B) with hand-designed patterns
such as format-based false negatives, and find that systematic false negatives
behave like random noise --- the model learns to avoid the triggering output and
still improves --- whereas false positives can cause plateau or collapse. Our
control tests the same question with a \emph{real} verifier whose bias we
measured, real data, and several supervised rounds, and reaches the same
conclusion.

\paragraph{Auditing self-improvement.}
Xu et al.~\cite{ref_phantom} audit three rounds of rank-32 LoRA self-training of
Qwen3-8B ($\eta=10^{-4}$; filters by answer match, majority vote or an external
teacher) against a frozen untrained control, and identify seven measurement
failures that each invert a reported finding when its control is absent,
including a fixed token cap under style drift and training-seed variance (the
corruption-to-learning ratio of one method spans $0.55$--$1.53$ across seeds).
Self-training does not improve rarely-solved problems ($0$--$2$ of $22$, against
$8$--$11$ for teacher distillation) and corrupts problems solved at baseline. This
is the study closest to ours in spirit, and our results meet theirs at three
points: the A-versus-B gap changes sign between seeds; a $150$-problem exam set
resolves only large effects; and self-training drifts in style, which costs the
adapters answers (Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}). They hold the filter
and learning rate fixed and use no measured format-biased verifier; we vary the
verifier and the learning rate and add reference-solution and no-filter controls,
but have no frozen control or distillation arm.

\paragraph{Reference-guided training.}
Kim et al.~\cite{ref_selfdistill} show that self-distillation conditioned on the
ground-truth solution can cut mathematical accuracy by up to $40\%$ (Qwen3-1.7B/8B,
DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B, Olmo3-7B-Instruct) while \emph{shortening} responses,
which they trace to suppressed epistemic verbalization. Our train-on-gold control
is a supervised, small-model analogue with the same signature (response length
$-59\%$, accuracy on finished solutions $37\%\to14\%$); we did not measure
epistemic markers, so we claim the signature, not the mechanism. Self-training
shows the \emph{opposite} length drift, which that literature does not predict.

\paragraph{Positioning.}
In the literature we surveyed (searched through October 2026) we found no study
combining a real, measured, format-biased rule-based verifier with several
sequential self-training rounds and a matched patched-verifier control. The
closest are Egashira et al.\ (hand-designed errors, one RLVR phase) and Xu et
al.\ (a measurement audit with answer, vote and teacher filters). We add that
combination, an account of how pooling, selection-set scoring and an undertrained
adapter each manufacture a misleading curve, and a decomposition of the decline
that remains.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{The GVT Loop}
\label{sec:loop}

Each round $t$ has four steps. \emph{Generate:} the current model samples $k$
solutions per problem at temperature $0.8$. \emph{Verify:} \texttt{math-verify}
extracts the final answer and compares it with the gold. \emph{Select:} accepted
solutions are kept. \emph{Train:} a \emph{fresh} LoRA adapter (rank $16$,
$\alpha=32$) is attached to the \emph{base} model and trained by supervised
fine-tuning on the kept solutions for one epoch; adapters are not accumulated, so
round $t$ learns only from round $t-1$'s accepted pool. Rounds are indexed from
$t=0$, the untrained base model. The first experiments use $k=8$ and five rounds.

\subsection{The Verifier and Its Directional Bias}
\label{sec:verifier}

\emph{Format probe.} We re-rendered gold answers from GSM8K and MATH-500 in
individually valid formats (bare number, trailing-zero decimal, \verb|\frac|,
\verb|\dfrac|, \verb|\tfrac|, boxed, dollar-delimited, sentence, with units) and
asked the verifier to grade them against the original gold. On GSM8K plain
rewrites are never rejected, while \verb|\dfrac|/\verb|\tfrac| are rejected
$200/200$ (the command is not parsed). On MATH-500, \verb|\dfrac|/\verb|\tfrac|
are rejected $146/146$ ($100\%$), trailing-zero decimals $59\%$, and
boxed/dollar-delimited forms about $20\%$ of the time.

\emph{False-negative rate on real solutions.} For genuinely correct public
model solutions (teacher traces for GSM8K, Qwen2.5-7B rollouts for MATH-500) we
checked manually, for each rejected solution, whether the boxed answer matched the
gold. The false-negative rate is $\mathbf{7.8\%}$ on MATH-500 ($10/128$) and
$\mathbf{0\%}$ on GSM8K ($0/200$), identical for the \texttt{default} and
\texttt{reward} presets. Of the ten MATH-500 cases, eight are wrong-token
extraction from a long solution and two are valid rewrites the verifier cannot
match; we never saw it accept an incorrect final answer.

\emph{The patch.} For the control we repaired the largest identified failure mode
with a normalisation pass that rewrites \verb|\dfrac| and \verb|\tfrac| to
\verb|\frac| before parsing, removing the $100\%$-rejection class and nothing else.

\subsection{Two Experimental Designs}
\label{sec:designs}

\emph{Design~P (pooled)} loads both datasets together and trains \emph{one}
adapter per round on the union of accepted solutions, with selection and
evaluation on the same problems. \emph{Design~I (isolated)} uses one dataset and
one adapter per run, and holds out $30\%$ of the problems ($150$ of $500$) as an
\texttt{exam} set never used for selection; all Design~I pass rates are on the exam
set. Table~\ref{tab:designs} summarises them.

\subsection{Models, Data, Training, Metrics}
\label{sec:config}

We use Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (Design~P uses both,
Design~I the 1.5B model only) and 500 problems each from GSM8K~\cite{ref_gsm8k}
and MATH-500, the 500-problem subset of MATH~\cite{ref_math}. Training is LoRA
supervised fine-tuning (\texttt{transformers}, \texttt{peft}, TRL
\texttt{SFTTrainer}), one epoch per round, at a learning rate of $10^{-5}$ unless
stated otherwise;\footnote{A post-hoc diagnostic shows this rate undertrains the
adapter (Section~\ref{sec:lrcheck} and Limitation~1).} we use SFT rather than
online RL because it matches the select-then-train structure of the loop. Metrics
per round are pass@1 and pass@$k$, style counts over all generations (boxed,
dfrac, dollar, \dots), pre-filter answer diversity (distinct normalised answers
among the $k$ samples), and the training loss on the kept pool. All runs use one
Kaggle T4 GPU; Design~P and Run~C were run once, Runs A and B at two seeds.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Design P: An Apparent Collapse}
\label{sec:pooled}

Table~\ref{tab:passk} reports pass@1 and pass@8 for both models over five rounds
of the pooled design (the two verifier presets give identical values). All series
decline, and the 1.5B decline is severe and accelerating. Splitting the 1.5B run
by dataset (Table~\ref{tab:bydataset}) sharpens the puzzle: under the original
hypothesis MATH-500 (false-negative rate $7.8\%$) should degrade and GSM8K
($0\%$) should hold, but both fall heavily and GSM8K loses more pass@1 in points.
The accepted pool also contracts every round (0.5B $-16\%$, 1.5B $-60\%$, from

\verb|\boxed{}| falling from $5{,}608$ to $2{,}693$ occurrences while
dollar-delimited math rises from $1{,}453$ to $3{,}406$. Read alone, this is a
coherent story of a shrinking, style-skewed pool driving capability down.

\subsection{Design I: The Collapse Does Not Reproduce at the Original Learning Rate}
\label{sec:isolated}

Table~\ref{tab:isolated} reports the isolated runs on their held-out exam sets.
The collapse is absent: pass@8 is flat in every run --- $57.3\%\to56.0\%$ and
$58.7\%\to58.7\%$ for Run~A at seeds 0 and 1, $57.3\%\to53.3\%$ for Run~B,
$92.7\%\to90.0\%$ for Run~C --- against declines of $22$--$26$ points in the
pooled design. On MATH-500 pass@1 falls about three points at round~1 and is flat
thereafter, and the two seeds agree ($-2.7$ and $-3.4$ points end to end);
rounds 1--4 show no trend in either direction. This is a one-off cost of the
first self-training round rather than a compounding process, which fits the
``amplifies but does not compound'' reading of~\cite{ref_teacherfree}. Run~C
retains a residual effect: GSM8K pass@1 falls $12.7$ points, almost all in the
final round ($63.3\%\to54.0\%$), while its pass@8 does not move; with $150$
problems and one seed we flag it as an open question, not a finding
(Section~\ref{sec:lrcheck} reruns it at a higher learning rate). The accepted
pools contract here as well (Run~C $-20\%$, Run~A $-3\%$) without any pass@8
decline, so pool contraction and collapse, which moved together in Design~P,
come apart.

\paragraph{Noise floor.}
\label{sec:seedvar}
Round~0 is the untrained base model, identical across seeds; only the exam
partition differs, yet round-0 pass@1 is $32.0\%$ at seed~0 and $36.7\%$ at
seed~1 ($4.7$ points, seven problems; pass@8 differs by $1.4$). This is a
between-partition variance. Most comparisons here are paired within a partition
(round~0 against round~4 in one run, A against B at one seed), where it largely
cancels, but it bounds comparisons of absolute levels across seeds, shows that a
$150$-problem exam set resolves differences of a few points only weakly --- the
regime of the A-versus-B gap of $0.6$ points --- and puts Run~C's $12.7$-point
decline outside what partition choice explains.

\subsection{The Control: Patching the Verifier, and Why It Changes Nothing}
\label{sec:control}
\label{sec:prevalence}

Runs A and B are identical in dataset, seed, split, model and training
configuration and differ only in whether \verb|\dfrac|/\verb|\tfrac| are
normalised before verification --- the one error class measured at $100\%$
rejection. The curves are indistinguishable (Table~\ref{tab:isolated}): after five
rounds pass@1 ends at $29.3\%$ unpatched against $28.7\%$ patched (one problem of
$150$) and pass@8 at $56.0\%$ against $53.3\%$, with Run~B oscillating
non-monotonically ($55.3\to52.0\to57.3\to53.3$); answer diversity and per-round
training loss are near-identical. At seed~1 the agreement is closer still: from
round~2 the two runs report identical pass@1 ($32.7\%$, $31.3\%$, $33.3\%$), and
across both seeds no round differs by more than $2.7$ points, with no consistent
sign. Within the precision of this experiment the hypothesis that directional
format bias drives multi-round degradation is \emph{not supported} at this
learning rate.

The reason is prevalence. At round~0, Runs A and B generate identical solutions
from the same base model, so the patch's direct scoring effect is isolated: zero
problems of $150$ at seed~0 and one at seed~1. Across the $4{,}000$ generations
per round on MATH-500, \verb|\dfrac| appears in $20$--$29$ (about $0.55\%$),
\verb|\tfrac| never, and on GSM8K neither occurs. The format probe's $100\%$ is
severity \emph{conditional on the format occurring}; what governs the training
pool is severity $\times$ prevalence, here $0.55\%$ of solutions wrongly
discarded per round, which cannot plausibly drive a twenty-point change. Our
audit agrees: of ten MATH-500 false negatives only two were format rewrites, and
eight were wrong-token extraction that the patch does not touch.
Verifier-bias figures in the literature, such as the $\sim$14\% of
\cite{ref_robustness} and the rejected fractions of \cite{ref_tinyv}, are
conditional severities measured on some reference distribution; whether they
matter depends on how often the affected forms occur in \emph{that pipeline's}
output, which is cheap to count and which we did not count until the hypothesis
had failed.

\subsection{Pre-Filter Answer Diversity Rises, It Does Not Collapse}
\label{sec:answer-diversity}

Does the model converge onto one \emph{answer} per problem before the verifier
sees the samples? We measured the mean fraction of distinct normalised answers
among the $k=8$ samples and the fraction of problems on which all $k$ agree.
Diversity rises in both designs, the opposite of a narrowing picture, and its
size tracks the pass@1 decline: all-$k$ agreement falls $24.8\%\to1.7\%$ for
Design~P's 1.5B run, $10.5\%\to7.3\%$ for 0.5B and $33.6\%\to16.0\%$ for Run~C,
but only $14.6\%\to12.0\%$ (A) and $14.6\%\to12.2\%$ (B) at seed~0, whose pass@1
barely moves. This fits a single underlying quantity, first-attempt reliability,
rather than stylistic convergence: a model that still solves a problem tends to
land on the same answer across attempts, while one whose reliability erodes
answers more erratically, which depresses pass@1 while leaving pass@8
comparatively intact.

larger at $5\times10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Anatomy of the Decline}
\label{sec:anatomy}

gets it right, and whether it finishes at all. We separate them on the exam
generations of every corrected-rate run. A generation is \emph{degenerate} if it
is longer than $1{,}500$ characters and contains no \verb|\boxed|; this is a
heuristic proxy for a response that never reached an answer within the $512$-token
generation cap, and its accuracy is $1.7$--$7.8\%$ in every run and round.

Table~\ref{tab:anatomy} shows two different failures. In all six self-training
runs at $5\times10^{-4}$ the degenerate share rises from $9$--$10\%$ to
$36$--$43\%$ and mean length grows by $40$--$50\%$, while accuracy on the

Holding the degenerate share at its round-0 level, Run~A at seed~1 would score
$35.7\%$ at round~2, above its round-0 $33.7\%$. The round-2 decline is therefore
mainly generations that do not finish, not a loss of accuracy on those that do
(the conditioning is descriptive: if harder problems are the ones that
degenerate, accuracy on the rest would rise without any change in ability). The
degenerate generations are not long legitimate solutions cut by the cap: they open
as ordinary solutions and drift into free-association text (``\ldots acknowledged
hurdles acknowledged setbacks acknowledged failures \ldots''), with low exact
repetition (mean 5-gram repeat ratio $0.02$, the same as finished generations), so
they are not loops. Train-on-gold is the opposite: responses \emph{shorten} by
$59\%$, the degenerate share vanishes, and accuracy on finished generations falls
from $37\%$ to $14\%$ --- a real loss of accuracy, in the direction Kim et
al.~\cite{ref_selfdistill} report. Across learning rates the drift is a dose--response
(Table~\ref{tab:lrseries}): the degenerate share at round~2 is $8.8\%$ at
$10^{-5}$, $19.5$--$22.7\%$ at $10^{-4}$ (three seeds), $22.2$--$25.0\%$ at

falls by $0.9$ (at $10^{-5}$, $k=8$), $2.0$, $3.7$ and $6.5$ points.

\paragraph{Two tests of the pipeline.}
The training limit (\texttt{max\_length}$=512$ tokens) equals the generation cap,
so $24$--$26\%$ of the kept pool ($108$ of $440$ examples in Run~A at round~0) is
truncated: it loses its final answer and end-of-sequence token and plausibly
teaches the model not to stop. We reran Run~A at seed~1 and $5\times10^{-4}$ with a
training limit of $1{,}024$ tokens, so that no kept example is truncated, and
re-scored the round-2 adapter of that run and of a matched replicate that keeps
the $512$-token limit on the same exam problems ($k=4$). The drift does not
fall: at the $512$-token generation cap the degenerate share is $37.2\%$ with the
longer limit against $30.0\%$ in the replicate ($600$ samples each, standard
error about $2$ points). Truncated training examples are therefore not what makes
the models drift, which also fits the no-filter run, whose pool is $62$--$68\%$
truncated and whose degenerate share ($38.8\%$) is no higher than that of the
filtered runs. The replicate also reproduces the decline of Run~A at seed~1
(pass@1 $33.3\to31.3\to25.3\%$, pass@4 $50.0\to48.0\to44.0\%$, against
$23.3\%$ and $42.7\%$ at round~2 in the first run).

Re-scoring the same adapters under a $1{,}536$-token cap, with everything else

untrained model gains as much from the larger cap as the adapters do (sample

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{sec:threevars}

An earlier version of this work explained the Design~P curves with a
self-reinforcing shrinking-pool mechanism: the biased verifier accepts a smaller,
more style-skewed set each round and the skew compounds. It is coherent and
matches Qiao et al.~\cite{ref_selectionbias} theoretically, but the isolated runs
do not support it. The pool contracts under Design~I as well ($20\%$ in Run~C)
with no pass@8 decline, and removing the bias that supposedly drives the skew
changes nothing (Section~\ref{sec:control}). What remains is narrower: in the
pooled design each dataset's update was dominated by the other's filtered data and
evaluation ran on the selection items, which suffices to produce the observed
curves without any appeal to verifier bias.

Attribution also needs a caveat on our own comparison. Design~I differs from
Design~P in three respects at once (isolation, a held-out split, and $n$ per run
falling from $1{,}000$ pooled problems to $500$), so we cannot apportion the
difference among them and do not claim that isolation alone removes the collapse.
The clean comparisons are internal to Design~I and to its corrected-rate
counterparts, where runs share every configuration except the one variable varied;
the verifier control rests on those, not on the P/I contrast. That control agrees
with Egashira et al.~\cite{ref_delayplateau}, who find that systematic false
negatives in one-round RLVR behave like random noise: two quite different setups
converging on ``systematic false negatives are tolerable'' is the most useful
positive statement this study can make.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

For GVT-style pipelines our experience suggests six precautions.
\emph{Do not pool datasets into one adapter} if you report per-dataset
trajectories: a mixture that is $69\%$ one domain gives per-domain results that
cannot be read independently, and isolation costs less than it appears.
\emph{Hold out evaluation items from selection}, the single change most likely to
manufacture a dramatic round-over-round curve.
\emph{Verify that the training step fits:} a flat curve from an adapter whose loss
does not fall is not evidence of stability, and a one-round diagnostic would have
shown it before any multi-round run.
\emph{Report length and termination, not only accuracy:} the accuracy of finished
solutions did not fall while the share of unfinished ones quadrupled, a style-drift
failure of the kind Xu et al.~\cite{ref_phantom} document.
\emph{Run the no-filter, train-on-gold and null controls:} a short SFT pass
degraded our model whatever the data were, and repairing the suspected defect and
re-running is the cheapest decisive experiment --- our central claim survived two
model sizes, tens of thousands of generations and a coherent mechanism story, and
failed the first time a single-variable control was run against it.
\emph{Track pass@$k$ alongside pass@1:} a rising pass@1 is not sufficient evidence
of improvement, and Run~C shows the converse, a falling pass@1 with flat pass@8
that would be misread as capability loss if pass@1 were the only metric.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The headline null result was obtained at a learning rate that
undertrains.} Every run in Sections~\ref{sec:isolated}--\ref{sec:control} uses
$10^{-5}$ (Section~\ref{sec:config}). A one-round diagnostic on the same MATH-500
pool, every other hyperparameter fixed, shows training loss oscillating between
$0.68$ and $0.84$ with no clear downward trend at $10^{-5}$ (six logged steps:
$0.836$, $0.729$, $0.678$, $0.737$, $0.699$, $0.724$), against a drop to the
$0.24$--$0.28$ range at $5\times10^{-4}$ ($0.626$, $0.354$, $0.242$, $0.240$,
$0.248$, $0.281$). The flat curves and the null control of Design~I were therefore
obtained with an adapter that barely moved; Section~\ref{sec:lrcheck} reruns the
loop at higher rates, and the Design~P/Design~I contrast itself was measured at
$10^{-5}$ only.
\item \emph{The corrected-rate evidence is thin.} Every corrected-rate run has
three rounds, $k=4$ and a $150$-problem exam set. A and B have two seeds at
$5\times10^{-4}$, A has three at $10^{-4}$ and two at $2\times10^{-4}$, and the
no-filter and train-on-gold controls have two; Run~C at the corrected rate and
the token-cap re-scoring are single seeds, and the learning-rate grid is coarse. The patched-versus-unpatched gap flips sign between seeds, so we find no
consistent patch effect, but cannot rule out a small one.
\item \emph{The anatomy of the decline rests on a heuristic.} A generation is
called degenerate if it is longer than $1{,}500$ characters and has no
\verb|\boxed|; accuracy on the remaining generations is descriptive, and
conditioning on it can raise accuracy without any change in ability if harder
problems are the ones that degenerate.
\item \emph{Limited seed replication at the original rate.} Runs A and B were run
at two seeds, which agree; Run~C and the pooled runs were run once, and Run~C's
$12.7$-point pass@1 decline is the one effect at $10^{-5}$ that exceeds the noise
floor.
\item \emph{Small exam set.} Design~I scores on $150$ held-out problems, where one
problem is $0.67$ points and re-partitioning alone moves round-0 pass@1 by $4.7$
points (Section~\ref{sec:seedvar}); differences of a few points, including the
entire A-versus-B gap, are below the resolution of this setup.
\item \emph{Three variables change between designs}
(Section~\ref{sec:threevars}), so the Design~P/Design~I contrast is suggestive
rather than attributive, and Design~I covers one model size, so we cannot say
whether the apparent ``larger collapses harder'' pattern of Design~P also
dissolves under isolation.
\item \emph{One patch, one bias class, one verifier, one training method.} Run~B
repairs \verb|\dfrac|/\verb|\tfrac| parsing, the error class measured at $100\%$
rejection, not the eight-of-ten false negatives caused by wrong-token extraction,
so the null concerns format-class bias specifically. Whether results change beyond
five rounds, under another verifier, or under full fine-tuning or online RL is
untested.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a directionally biased rule-based verifier causes capability
collapse when used as the filter in a multi-round GVT loop. Pooled experiments
scored on the selection items produced a dramatic collapse, but isolating each
dataset, holding out an exam split and adding a matched patched-verifier control
removes it at our training rate, and the control is null at two seeds. The reason
is prevalence: the affected format is about half a percent of generations, so a
$100\%$ rejection rate discards half a percent of the training pool.

That rate was too low to train the adapter. At $10^{-4}$ the adapter trains and
accuracy falls by about two points over three seeds, within noise; at
$5\times10^{-4}$ all four runs, patched and unpatched, lose accuracy by round~2,
and the loss grows with the learning rate.
The verifier control survives in a weaker form: the patch's effect flips sign
between seeds, the decline occurs with no filter at all, and training on
reference solutions is worse, at both seeds of each control; on GSM8K the decline
at $5\times10^{-4}$ is larger still. What the decline is made of differs by regime
(Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}): under self-training it is mostly generations that
drift and never answer, with accuracy on finished solutions unchanged, not caused
by truncated training examples, and not removed by tripling the generation cap
(the untrained model gains as much from it); under reference-solution training it
is shortened responses and a real loss of accuracy. Read as accuracy under a fixed
token cap, the first looks like lost reasoning, the measurement trap Xu et al.\
warn of, although here the unfinished answers are lost at any cap.

The finding we can defend is therefore methodological rather than mechanistic.
Pooling and selection-set scoring together suffice to produce a collapse
signature that isolation and held-out evaluation do not reproduce; an undertrained
adapter produces reassuringly flat curves; and accuracy alone hides whether a
model finishes its answers. Each was found only because a control was run against
it, and we report the reversals in full because the designs that produced them
are not unusual.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Characterise the generations that still never answer under a large cap (they open
normally and drift into unrelated text) and test whether decoding settings or an
end-of-sequence penalty remove them; refine the learning-rate grid between $10^{-4}$ and $5\times10^{-4}$ and replicate
Run~C (GSM8K) at further seeds and rates; extend the corrected-rate runs to five rounds with $k=8$ so they compare
directly with Table~\ref{tab:isolated}; and extend the isolated design to a second
model size.

A second format-class defect gives a natural next control. \texttt{math-verify}'s
rich \LaTeX{} extraction only activates inside a recognised delimiter
(\verb|\boxed{...}| or \verb|$...$|), while our gold answers are stored
undelimited, so a correctly boxed compound answer can be compared against a
truncated gold stub. The format probe attributes $17/41$ of MATH-500
\texttt{boxed} rejections to this mechanism ($8.5\%$; the other $24/41$ are
pre-existing unparseable gold formats), an estimated damage of $8.5\%\times51.5\%
\approx4.4\%$ of solutions under the measured prevalence --- roughly eight times
\verb|\dfrac|'s $0.55\%$ and untested by any study we know of. We implemented and
unit-tested a patch (wrap gold in \verb|\boxed{}| whenever the prediction contains
one and gold does not) and queue it as a Run-D control against the headline
configuration, as Run~B was against Run~A.
